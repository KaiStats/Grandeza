Executive readout

Five findings, in the order a plaintiff's lawyer would find them.

Ranked by how quickly each one turns into a demand letter — not by how hard it is to fix. Three of these are platform-level: they live in the flow template, not in the shop.

01

A spoken "stop calling me" goes nowhere.

In a 500-call transcript sample, 11 callers revoked consent out loud — "take me off the list," "quit texting me about that." The agent acknowledged all 11 warmly and wrote none of them to a suppression list. Revocation is valid in any reasonable manner, and telling the AI counts. Continued contact after a revocation the record shows you heard is the cleanest TCPA claim there is: the transcript is the plaintiff's exhibit, and you produced it.

02

Service consent is being spent on marketing.

"Is this a good number to text you?" earns an informational text about tomorrow's 7–9am window. It does not earn the Comfort Club offer sent 40 seconds later. Marketing to a wireless number needs prior express written consent with the required disclosures — a spoken yes on a service call isn't it. One booking flow produces both messages, so the violation scales with the shop's best month.

CTRL-05 FAILCTRL-01 PARTIAL47 C.F.R. §64.1200(f)(9)
03

The outbound AI voice is a regulated artifact.

Since February 2024 the FCC has treated an AI-generated voice as an "artificial voice" under the TCPA. That puts every outbound reactivation call in the consent-required column — and California has required since January 2025 that an artificial voice be disclosed at the start of the call. Cardinal's quarterly reactivation list is built from a CRM query with no DNC scrub, no suppression join and no time-zone guard, then dialed by an AI voice that never says it's one.

04

Every call is recorded. Nobody is told.

The agent transcribes and retains audio by design — that's how it books accurately. But the shop takes calls from all-party-consent states, and there's no recording disclosure anywhere in the greeting. California's wiretap statute carries $5,000 per violation with a private right of action, and it's currently one of the most heavily litigated theories against conversational AI.

05

There's a record of everything and evidence of nothing.

Transcripts sit in the agent platform, jobs in Housecall Pro, messages at the carrier, and none of them are joined by a consent identifier or tamper-evident. When a demand letter arrives with a four-year lookback, the shop can produce a lot of text and prove none of it. Same facts, two outcomes: a defensible file closes at nuisance value; an undocumented one gets certified.

Remediation

The fix is a gate in front of the send, not a new stack.

Nothing below replaces the agent, the CRM or the carrier. One call before each outbound touch returns allow/deny plus the disclosures that touch requires, and writes the decision to an append-only record. Deny costs a contact. It also costs $500 less than the alternative.

As deployed — quarterly reactivation 
# scheduler → CRM query → dial. No gate anywhere.
on schedule("quarterly_reactivation"):
    contacts = crm.query("last_service > 180d")
    for c in contacts:
        newo.call(c.phone, flow="reactivation_offer")
        sms.send(c.phone, template="comfort_club")

# 6,200 contacts. 0 registry lookups. 0 suppression
# joins. 0 local-time checks. 0 consent bindings.
# 0 disclosures on an artificial voice.
Hardened — same flow, one gate 
# The agent is untouched. The send is gated.
on schedule("quarterly_reactivation"):
    contacts = crm.query("last_service > 180d")
    for c in contacts:
        gate = grandeza.check(
            phone   = c.phone,
            purpose = "marketing",      # vs "service"
            channel = "voice",
            local   = tz.of(c.phone),
            origin  = shop.state)

        if not gate.allow:
            audit.log(gate)   # dnc | revoked |
            # window | no_pewc — the deny is evidence too
            continue

        newo.call(c.phone, flow="reactivation_offer",
            preamble = gate.disclosures,  # AI + recording
            consent  = gate.token)        # binds send↔proof

Why this is a platform conversation

Cardinal has one broken flow. A platform has one broken flow, N times.

The defect replicates

Every finding here lives in the template, not in the tenant. A greeting without a disclosure ships to each new shop in three minutes — that's the product's best feature working exactly as designed. One template fix inoculates the installed base; one tenant fix inoculates one shop.

Tenants can't fix it themselves

An HVAC owner cannot add revocation capture to a shared flow, and shouldn't have to know it's missing. The compliance surface is created at the platform layer, so it has to be closed there — or every tenant reinvents it badly.

Liability doesn't stop at the tenant

TCPA plaintiffs routinely name every party in the calling chain and litigate the platform's role. Whatever the eventual allocation, the discovery, the fees and the news coverage land on the name in the caller ID and the name on the technology.

It's a reason to buy, not a warning label

"Books your jobs at 2am" is table stakes now. "Books your jobs at 2am, with a consent record your insurer accepts" is a line no competitor can copy in a sprint — and it's the answer when an enterprise franchise group's counsel asks the one question that stalls the deal.

Regulatory basis

What each finding rests on.

Current as of July 2026 and stated plainly. Compliance engineering, not legal advice — confirm the live status of anything under waiver with your counsel before relying on it.

FCC Declaratory Ruling 24-17February 8, 2024
An AI-generated voice is an "artificial voice" under the TCPA. Outbound AI voice calls sit in the consent-required column — prior express consent, and prior express written consent where the purpose is marketing.
47 C.F.R. §64.1200(a)(9)–(10)revocation rules · eff. April 11, 2025
Consumers may revoke in any reasonable manner; revocation must be honored within ten business days; one confirmatory message is permitted. Parts of the cross-business-unit "revoke-all" element have been subject to limited FCC waivers — check current status.
Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC11th Cir., January 24, 2025
Vacated the one-to-one consent rule before it took effect. Consent for AI/prerecorded marketing calls reverts to the prior written-consent standard — a lower bar than the vacated rule, and still a bar Cardinal doesn't clear.
Cal. Pub. Util. Code §2874 (AB 2905)eff. January 1, 2025
A call using an AI-generated or AI-altered voice must disclose that at the beginning of the call. Enforced by the CPUC; no private right of action, which is not the same as no consequence.
Cal. Penal Code §§632.7, 637.2 · Nev. Rev. Stat. §200.620all-party recording consent
Recording a call without all-party consent in these states is actionable, with $5,000 statutory damages per violation and a private right of action in California — currently a heavily litigated theory against conversational-AI vendors.
47 C.F.R. §64.1200(c), (d), (f)(5)windows · internal DNC · EBR
No solicitation before 8am or after 9pm in the called party's local time; internal do-not-call requests honored for five years; established business relationship runs 18 months from a transaction, 3 months from an inquiry — which is what expires under a 180-day reactivation query.
28 U.S.C. §1658 · 47 U.S.C. §227(b)(3)limitations & damages
Four-year limitations period; $500 per violation, up to $1,500 where the violation is willful or knowing. The four years is why retention and producibility are controls, not paperwork.
State AI disclosure statutesUT, CO, TX and others · 2025–2026
A widening patchwork of disclosure and high-risk-use obligations for AI systems that interact with consumers. The practical implication for a multi-state deployment: disclosure behavior has to be state-aware at the flow level, not a global toggle.
