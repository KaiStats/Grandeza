Does the TCPA Apply to My Shop?

Yes — if you text or call customers using any kind of automation, the TCPA applies to you. Appointment reminders, "tech is on the way" texts, review requests, seasonal tune-up blasts, and AI phone agents all count. The law does not have a small-business exemption, and plaintiffs' firms know that a 12-truck shop is easier to pressure into a settlement than a Fortune 500 company.

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act is a 1991 federal law, enforced by the FCC and — this is the important part — by private lawsuits. Any person who receives a message that violates the law can sue you directly. No regulator has to get involved. No harm has to be shown beyond receiving the message.

Here's a simple test. Does your shop do any of the following?

  • Send automated appointment or maintenance reminders through ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, or similar software
  • Send "your technician is 20 minutes out" dispatch texts
  • Send review requests after a job closes
  • Run seasonal marketing texts ("Book your AC tune-up before summer")
  • Use an AI voice agent or answering service to make or return calls
  • Call or text old customer lists to win back business

If you checked even one box, you're in TCPA territory. That's not a reason to panic — most of these activities are perfectly legal when done correctly. The rest of this guide covers what "correctly" means.

What Changed in 2025–2026?

Three things changed the game: a federal appeals court threw out the FCC's strictest consent rule, a major revocation rule got pushed back, and plaintiffs' lawyers found a new favorite weapon — quiet-hours claims aimed squarely at small businesses. Litigation is up sharply: compliance firm CompliancePoint reports 2,628 TCPA lawsuits filed in 2025, roughly a 60% increase year over year.

The one-to-one consent rule was struck down

The FCC's "one-to-one consent" rule would have required lead generators to get separate consent for each individual business a lead might be sold to. In January 2025, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals struck it down in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC (ca11.uscourts.gov), one day before it was set to take effect. What this means for you: if you buy leads, the loosest interpretation of consent is back in play for lead sellers — but you still carry the risk when you call those leads. Sloppy lead vendors get you sued; the ruling didn't change that.

The "revocation applies to everything" rule was delayed

The FCC's rule that a single opt-out revokes consent for all automated calls and texts from your business — not just the campaign the customer replied to — has been delayed to January 2027 (fcc.gov). Don't wait for the deadline. If a customer texts STOP to your marketing line and your reminder system keeps texting them, you're building a plaintiff's exhibit list. The core revocation requirements already in effect are strict: you must honor opt-outs made by any reasonable method, within ten business days.

The quiet-hours lawsuit wave

Serial plaintiffs discovered that texts sent before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time zone can support claims even against businesses the recipient did business with. Small contractors — who often have automated systems firing at odd hours — became prime targets. More on this in the quiet hours section below.

What Are the Four Things I Must Be Able to Prove?

TCPA compliance comes down to four things you must be able to prove, not just do: that you had consent, that you screened against do-not-call lists, that you honored opt-outs, and that you kept records of all three. In a lawsuit, "we always ask permission" is worth nothing. A timestamped record of the exact form a customer submitted is worth everything.

  1. Consent. For every number you contact with automation, you need the right level of consent for the type of message (see the consent section). You need to know when it was captured, where, and what the customer actually saw.
  2. DNC screening. Before any marketing call or text, numbers must be checked against the National Do Not Call Registry and your own internal do-not-call list. Existing-customer relationships create limited exceptions, but the safest posture is: screen everything you market to.
  3. Opt-out honoring. STOP replies, verbal requests to a tech, an email saying "quit texting me" — all of them count, and all of them must actually shut off future automated messages. The most common small-business violation is an opt-out captured in one system while three other systems keep texting.
  4. Audit trail. Consent records, message logs, opt-out timestamps, DNC scrub records. If you can produce these within a day of a demand letter, most cases die early. If you can't, you're negotiating from weakness.

What Does a TCPA Violation Actually Cost?

The statute sets damages at $500 per violation — per message, not per person — and up to $1,500 per message for willful violations. There is no cap, which is why a routine text campaign can become a company-ending number. Even if you win, defending a TCPA class action typically costs $50,000 or more in legal fees.

Do the math on a real scenario. Your system sends a $40 diagnostic-fee promotion to an old customer list of 1,000 numbers. Two hundred of those people never gave written consent for marketing texts. That's 200 violations at $500 each — $100,000 in exposure from one send. If the plaintiff argues you knew better (you'd received STOP replies before, say), it's $300,000.

Plaintiffs' firms don't need a big company. They need a big list and a missing consent record.

There's also the cost nobody budgets for: time. Discovery means producing every message log, every consent record, every vendor contract. For an owner-operator, months of that is its own penalty.

How Does Consent Work in Practice?

The TCPA has two tiers of consent, and knowing which tier a message falls into is most of the job. Transactional messages about an existing job need prior express consent — usually satisfied when the customer gives you their number. Marketing messages sent with automation need prior express written consent, which most shops have never properly collected.

Transactional messages

These are messages about a service the customer already requested: appointment confirmations, reschedule notices, "tech is en route" texts, invoices. When a customer gives you their number to book a job, that's generally consent for automated messages about that job. Keep them clean. The moment a transactional text includes "and ask about our maintenance plan!" — you've turned it into a marketing message that needed written consent.

Marketing messages

Tune-up promotions, membership upsells, win-back campaigns, financing offers. Sent with automation, these require prior express written consent: a signed agreement (an unchecked-by-default checkbox on your booking form counts as a signature under the E-SIGN Act) that names your business, discloses that messages are automated, and states that consent isn't required to buy anything.

What a compliant booking-form consent line looks like

☐ I agree to receive automated service and marketing calls and text messages from [Your Company Name] at the phone number provided, including via autodialer and prerecorded or artificial voice. Message frequency varies; message and data rates may apply. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Reply STOP to cancel or HELP for help. See our Privacy Policy.

Three details matter: the checkbox must not be pre-checked, the language must be visible near the submit button (not buried in a linked document), and you must store a record of exactly what the customer saw and when they submitted it. Have your attorney review your final language.

Does Using AI Change Anything?

Yes. The FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices are "artificial voices" under the TCPA (fcc.gov), which means AI voice calls trigger the law's strictest consent requirements. If you bolted an AI agent onto your phone line, you expanded your TCPA surface area — often without realizing it.

Here's the distinction that matters for your shop:

  • Inbound AI answering — an AI agent picking up when customers call you — is generally low risk. The customer initiated the contact.
  • Outbound AI calls — the agent calling customers to confirm appointments, follow up on estimates, or chase unsold quotes — are artificial-voice calls. Marketing-flavored outbound AI calls require prior express written consent, and that consent should specifically cover artificial or prerecorded voice.

Disclosure matters too. Regulators and several state laws are converging on the expectation that an AI caller identifies itself as such and identifies your business at the start of the call. An AI agent that sounds human and doesn't say so is a liability multiplier: it makes willfulness easier to argue, which triples the per-call damages.

One more trap: many AI phone platforms will happily call any list you upload, at any hour, with no consent checks. The platform's terms of service put the compliance burden on you. "The software let me do it" is not a defense — it's an admission.

What's the Deal With Quiet Hours?

Federal rules prohibit telephone solicitations before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time zone. In 2025, plaintiffs' firms industrialized this into a lawsuit wave: serial plaintiffs sign up for texts, wait for one to arrive at 7:58 a.m. or 9:04 p.m. their time, and file. Courts are split on whether having consent defeats these claims, which makes them cheap to file and expensive to fight.

The trap for contractors is time zones. Your shop is in Dallas; your snowbird customer's cell number has a Florida area code but they're currently in Arizona. Which "local time" applies? Plaintiffs argue it's wherever they physically are — something you can't know. While the FCC has been asked to clarify the rule (fcc.gov), the litigation continues, and small businesses with automated reminders firing on fixed schedules are disproportionately hit.

Practical defenses that work today:

  • Restrict all automated marketing sends to a conservative window — 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. — based on the number's area code and any service address you have on file.
  • Audit your platform's queue behavior. Some systems queue overnight messages and dump them at midnight or the moment the system restarts. That single misconfiguration has produced entire class actions.
  • Keep transactional messages transactional. Quiet-hour rules target solicitations; a genuine "your tech is arriving at 8:15 a.m." text sent at 7:50 a.m. is a much harder case for a plaintiff — unless you stuffed a promo into it.

What About State Mini-TCPAs?

More than a dozen states have their own telemarketing laws, and several are stricter than federal law. Florida's Telephone Solicitation Act tightened quiet hours to 8 a.m.–8 p.m. and limits contact attempts per day; Oklahoma's Telephone Solicitation Act closely mirrors Florida's, with its own private right of action; and Washington's updated law expands consumer suit rights around commercial texts. If you operate in — or text customers who live in — these states, the stricter rule wins. The practical takeaway fits in one sentence: build your consent capture, send windows, and opt-out handling to the strictest standard you touch, and the state-by-state differences mostly take care of themselves.

How Do I Make My Intake Provably Compliant?

The goal isn't just to be compliant — it's to be able to prove you're compliant in the first 48 hours after a demand letter arrives. That proof is what turns a six-figure settlement negotiation into a one-page response from your lawyer. Grandeza's framework for getting there has four steps: scan, score, harden, prove.

Step 1: Scan

Inventory every way your business touches a customer's phone: booking forms, chat widgets, field software, texting platforms, AI agents, answering services, review tools, and marketing lists. Most owners find two or three channels they forgot existed — usually the ones a vendor set up.

Step 2: Score

Measure each channel against the 16 Controls — the specific, checkable requirements that determine whether you'd survive a demand letter. They break into four groups:

  • Consent Capture (Controls 1–4): compliant written-consent language, no pre-checked boxes, transactional/marketing separation, consent records stored with timestamps.
  • List Hygiene (Controls 5–8): National DNC scrubbing, internal do-not-call list, lead-vendor consent verification, stale-list retirement.
  • Send Controls (Controls 9–12): quiet-hours enforcement by recipient time zone, frequency limits, AI/artificial-voice disclosure, platform queue-behavior verification.
  • Proof & Response (Controls 13–16): cross-platform opt-out synchronization, complete message logs, a maintained compliance binder, and a demand-letter response plan.

Step 3: Harden

Fix the failed controls in priority order. Consent language and opt-out synchronization come first — they're the two failures behind most small-business TCPA cases. Send-window and DNC fixes are usually configuration changes you can make in an afternoon.

Step 4: Prove

Assemble the compliance binder: your consent language with screenshots and version history, DNC scrub logs, opt-out records, send-window settings, and vendor attestations, all in one place and kept current. This is the document your attorney reaches for when the demand letter arrives — and the reason it gets answered instead of settled.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do appointment reminders need consent?

Yes, but only prior express consent — not the stricter written kind. When a customer gives you their number while booking, that generally covers automated texts about that job. Keep reminders purely transactional; the moment you add a promotion, you need prior express written consent.

Can I text a customer who called me first?

Generally yes, for messages about the reason they called. A customer who calls to book a repair has given prior express consent for texts related to that service. That consent doesn't extend to marketing blasts later, and it can be revoked at any time.

What is prior express written consent?

A signed, written agreement — an online form checkbox counts — in which the customer clearly agrees to receive automated marketing calls or texts from your business at a specific number, and is told consent isn't a condition of purchase. You need it before any automated marketing message.

Do I need a lawyer?

If you've been sued or received a demand letter, yes, immediately. For prevention, most shops can get compliant with a structured checklist — consent language, DNC screening, opt-outs, quiet hours, and records — then have an attorney review the final consent language.

What about my answering service?

Their calls and texts are your liability. Courts routinely hold businesses responsible for vendors acting on their behalf. Ask how they capture consent, whether they screen against DNC lists, and whether they honor quiet hours — and get it in writing.

How do I check my DNC status?

Register at telemarketing.donotcall.gov to access the National Do Not Call Registry and scrub your outbound marketing numbers. Access to up to five area codes is free. You must also keep your own internal do-not-call list of anyone who asks you to stop.

What if I already got a demand letter?

Don't reply, don't delete anything, and call a TCPA defense attorney before responding. Preserve all message logs, consent records, and platform data immediately. Demand letters from serial plaintiffs often resolve for far less when you can produce consent and opt-out records quickly.

How often should I re-check?

Quarterly at minimum, and immediately after any tooling change — a new booking form, texting platform, AI agent, or campaign. The rules keep moving: 2025 and 2026 alone rewrote federal consent and revocation requirements.
